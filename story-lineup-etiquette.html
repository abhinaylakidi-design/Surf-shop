Good Waves, Good Company: Lineup Etiquette

Surfing happens in shared space. Etiquette is the everyday practice of paying attention to the people around you and making decisions that keep a session considerate and predictable.

01 /

Begin by choosing a suitable place

Good intentions cannot compensate for being in conditions beyond your current ability. Before thinking about where to sit in a lineup, discuss an appropriate learning area with an instructor. The demands of a crowded break can be very different from those of a supervised beginner session, even when both appear inviting from the beach.

Watch how the area is being used and read any posted instructions. Different locations may have designated zones, restrictions, or particular hazards. Do not assume that being able to paddle out means you are ready to participate comfortably. If you cannot yet manage your board consistently or understand what nearby surfers are doing, seek more guidance in a suitable setting. Choosing an appropriate place gives you enough attention to learn, rather than spending the entire session trying to keep up with situations you do not yet understand.

02 /

Observe before joining the movement

From shore, notice where waves are being ridden and how people move around the area. Once you are in an appropriate supervised session, keep listening to your instructor about positioning. A space that appears empty for a moment may be part of someone's path a few seconds later. Learning to notice movement is more useful than memorizing a single place to remain.

Avoid treating every opening as an invitation to move closer to the busiest point. Give yourself room to understand what is happening, and ask questions when the pattern is unclear. Observation also includes noticing your own state: fatigue and frustration can narrow your attention. Taking a break can improve the next part of a session more than trying to force another attempt. Being considerate begins with having enough awareness left to notice other people.

03 /

Understand dropping in through instruction

Dropping in generally means taking off into the path of someone who already has priority on the wave. It can interfere with their ride and create a collision risk. Priority is commonly related to positioning near the breaking part of a wave, but a short written rule does not explain every situation, especially at different breaks or with multiple riders approaching.

Ask an instructor to explain the examples that apply to your practice setting. Do not assume that shouting makes a disputed wave yours, or that another surfer will always move out of your way. If you are uncertain about a situation, choose patience rather than a contest. The useful lesson is broader than winning a particular turn: pay attention before committing, understand the local arrangement, and avoid making someone else's ride depend on them correcting your decision at the last moment.

04 /

Give boards and people room

A surfboard occupies more space than the person riding it, and its movement does not stop instantly when a ride ends. Be aware of the people around your equipment during instruction, entry, practice, and exit. Ask your instructor how to manage the board for the conditions and equipment you are using, rather than copying a maneuver from a video.

Do not rely on a leash as permission to let the board go near others. Keep listening to guidance about spacing and board control, and move away from situations you cannot manage appropriately. On shore, the same consideration applies while carrying the board, resting it, or removing equipment. Crowded access points can become awkward when people stop without looking behind them. Giving room is a simple principle that matters throughout the day, not only during the moment someone stands up on a wave.

05 /

Communicate briefly and respectfully

Clear, calm communication is more useful than a running commentary. Follow the calls and signals explained by your instructor and ask about anything you do not recognize. Avoid assuming that everyone can hear you over wind and surf. Your actions should remain considerate even when a shouted message does not reach the other person.

If you make a mistake, acknowledge it without turning the conversation into an argument about who deserved the wave. Check whether the other person is all right when appropriate, listen, and give space. If an interaction becomes aggressive, prioritize disengaging and seek help from your instructor or relevant local staff. A session is not improved by escalating a disagreement. Respectful behavior also includes allowing other beginners the patience you would appreciate while you are still learning.

06 /

Make restraint part of progress

As your confidence grows, it is easy to measure improvement only by how many waves you attempt. Add other questions to your review: Did you understand the practice area? Did you leave room for others? Did you recognize a moment when waiting was the better choice? These observations tell you something about how comfortably you are participating in shared water.

Discuss uncertain moments after the session while they are still easy to describe. An instructor can often explain a situation that felt confusing in real time. Keep treating etiquette as a skill to practice rather than a list you have finished learning. The RNLI surfing guidance linked below is a useful starting reference, alongside local instruction. A satisfying session gives you something to learn from and leaves the people around you room to enjoy their own time in the ocean.

TAKE IT TO THE WATER

A few things to remember.

  • Choose conditions and a practice area suited to your ability.
  • Observe movement before committing to a wave.
  • Ask for practical instruction on priority and board control.
  • Acknowledge mistakes calmly and give others room.

Further reading: RNLI surfing safety guidance. Follow local guidance for your own coastline.
